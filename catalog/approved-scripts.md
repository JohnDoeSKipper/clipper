## ophelia (paintings, 2026-11-27)

Angle: Why did painting a drowning nearly make its model seriously ill?
Logline: Millais kept his model in a cold bath, so why such lengths? The Pre-Raphaelites swore to paint nature exactly, so he painted the riverbank outdoors for five months, but Ophelia had to be painted indoors in winter, so Siddal floated in a bath warmed by lamps that went out, so she fell ill and he paid the doctor, but the real story is the flowers, exact, symbolic and impossible together, but Siddal herself died young ten years later, so every petal is real and the drowning was nearly real too.
On-screen hook: A MODEL IN A COLD BATH

[hook] Millais kept his model in a cold bath to paint this drowning.
[hook / so] She fell seriously ill, and her father threatened to sue.
[question / so] So why go to such lengths for a scene from Hamlet?
[reveal1 / answer] Millais, 22, was a Pre-Raphaelite, sworn to paint nature exactly as it is.
[reveal1 / so] So he painted this riverbank outdoors for five months, eleven hours a day.
[reveal2 / but] But Ophelia herself was painted indoors, in winter.
[reveal2 / detail] Elizabeth Siddal, 19, floated in a bath, warmed by oil lamps underneath.
[reveal2 / but] One day the lamps went out, and Millais didn't notice.
[reveal2 / so] She caught a severe cold, and he paid her doctor's bills.
[reveal3 / but] But the real story is in the flowers.
[reveal3 / detail] Poppies for death, violets for faithfulness, pansies for love in vain.
[reveal3 / but] They bloom in different seasons: no real riverbank holds them all at once.
[reveal3 / detail] Even the robin echoes her song: bonny sweet Robin is all my joy.
[turn / but] But Siddal's own story ended young: she died of a laudanum overdose at 32.
[payoff / so] So every petal is real, and the drowning came close to real too.

## newcombs-paradox (philosophy, 2026-11-27)

Angle: If a near-perfect predictor has already filled the boxes, should you take one box or both?
Logline: A near-perfect predictor has filled the boxes, so one or two? A physicist at a weapons lab set the rule: a million only if you'll take one box, but the money is already there, so two boxes always earns more, but one-boxers walk away rich, so when Nozick published it the world split almost in half, but predictors are now real, so the question becomes whether a predictable choice is still yours.
On-screen hook: $1,000 OR $1,000,000?

[hook] A glass box holds 1,000 dollars; the closed box, maybe a million.
[hook / detail] A machine that's almost never wrong has already decided what's inside.
[question / so] So do you take both boxes, or just the closed one?
[reveal1 / answer] A physicist at a California weapons lab invented this puzzle around 1960.
[reveal1 / detail] The rule: if the machine predicted you'd take only the closed box, it holds the million.
[reveal1 / detail] If it predicted you'd take both, the closed box is empty.
[reveal2 / but] But the money is already in place; nothing you do now can change it.
[reveal2 / so] So taking both always gets you 1,000 dollars more. Logic says: two boxes.
[reveal2 / but] But people who take one box almost always walk away millionaires.
[reveal3 / so] So when Robert Nozick published it in 1969, the world split.
[reveal3 / detail] Almost everyone, he wrote, finds the answer obvious, and the other half silly.
[reveal3 / detail] In a 2016 online poll of 31,000 people, 53 percent took one box.
[reveal3 / but] Among professional philosophers in 2020, two boxes led, 39 to 31.
[turn / but] But predictors are no longer science fiction: algorithms already forecast what you'll click and buy.
[payoff / so] So the real question isn't the money; it's whether a choice that can be predicted is still yours.
[payoff / so] One box, or two?

## lifeboat (philosophy, 2026-11-27)

Angle: If killing one person could save three, is it still murder?
Logline: Poe imagined castaways eating Richard Parker, and in 1884 it happened to a real Richard Parker, so is killing one to save three murder? The Mignonette sank, four men drifted 19 days, so the captain killed the dying cabin boy, but they hid nothing, because sailors trusted a custom of the sea, but the court made it a test case and ruled necessity no defence, because no one may judge whose life is worth less, but the Crown cut the death sentence to six months, so law said murder and mercy said six months.
On-screen hook: POE WROTE IT FIRST

[hook] In 1838, Poe wrote about castaways eating a boy named Richard Parker.
[hook / but] In 1884, castaways really did, and his name was Richard Parker.
[question / so] So if killing one could save three, is it still murder?
[reveal1 / answer] The yacht Mignonette sank in the South Atlantic that July.
[reveal1 / detail] Four men drifted in a small dinghy with two tins of turnips.
[reveal1 / detail] After 19 days, the 17-year-old cabin boy, sick from drinking seawater, lay dying.
[reveal1 / so] So the captain killed him, and the three men ate him.
[reveal2 / but] But they didn't hide it.
[reveal2 / detail] Rescued days later, they told the police in Falmouth everything.
[reveal2 / because] Sailors believed in a custom of the sea: in extremity, someone must die.
[reveal2 / so] Most of Falmouth took their side, and the sailor who had refused to join in was never charged.
[reveal3 / but] But the court turned it into a test case.
[reveal3 / detail] The judges ruled that necessity is no defence to murder.
[reveal3 / because] Who is to judge, they asked, whose life is worth less? And the boy had drawn no lot.
[reveal3 / so] So both men were sentenced to death.
[turn / but] But the Crown cut it to six months in prison.
[turn / detail] The captain moved to Sydney, and in 1900 became the city's first victim of bubonic plague.
[payoff / so] So the law said murder, and mercy said six months, both at once.
[payoff / so] Adrift and starving, would you have drawn lots?

## achilles-tortoise (philosophy, 2026-11-27)

Angle: Where does Zeno's argument that Achilles can never catch the tortoise go wrong?
Logline: By Zeno's logic the fastest hero never catches a tortoise, so where's the flaw? Zeno posed it to prove motion is an illusion, but Achilles obviously wins, so the question is what's wrong with the steps, which shrink forever, but infinitely many steps can add to a finite distance, 111 and a ninth metres, made airtight only in the 1800s, but philosophers say that answers how far, not how infinitely many tasks get done, and physicists named an effect after him, so Zeno lost the race and shaped the maths of infinity.
On-screen hook: HE NEVER CATCHES IT?

[hook] By this logic, the fastest Greek hero never catches a tortoise.
[hook / because] Each time Achilles reaches where it was, it has crawled a little further.
[question / so] So where exactly does the argument go wrong?
[reveal1 / answer] Zeno of Elea posed it around 450 BC.
[reveal1 / detail] His book is lost; we know it mainly through Aristotle, who tried to refute it.
[reveal1 / detail] Zeno wasn't joking: he wanted to show that motion itself is an illusion.
[reveal2 / but] But obviously Achilles wins: legend says one philosopher answered by simply walking away.
[reveal2 / so] So the real puzzle is what's wrong with the steps.
[reveal2 / detail] Give the tortoise 100 metres; he's ten times faster.
[reveal2 / detail] The gaps shrink: 10 metres, then 1, then a tenth, forever.
[reveal3 / but] But infinitely many steps can add up to a finite distance.
[reveal3 / detail] They total 111 and one-ninth metres; he passes it right there.
[reveal3 / detail] Mathematicians only made that airtight in the 1800s, with the idea of a limit.
[turn / but] But some philosophers say the maths tells you how far, not how anyone finishes infinitely many tasks.
[turn / detail] And in 1990, physicists named an effect after him: watch an atom often enough, and it freezes.
[payoff / so] So Zeno lost the race, and still shaped the maths of infinity.
[payoff / so] If you can never finish an infinite list, how do you ever cross a room?

Angle: How did Van Gogh paint a night scene without any black?
Logline: His first starry sky has no black, so how do you paint darkness? On-screen hook: NIGHT, WITHOUT BLACK

[hook] Van Gogh's first starry sky has no black in it.
[hook / detail] A night painting without black, he told his sister: only blue, violet and green.
[question / so] So how do you paint darkness without dark paint?
[reveal1 / answer] In September 1888, he set up his easel on a square in Arles, at night.

[payoff / so] So the darkness is built from colour, and the real café has painted itself yellow to match.

## survivorship-bias (philosophy, 2026-11-27)

Angle: Why do the cats that fall farthest seem to get hurt least?
Logline: Cats from higher floors seemed to get hurt less, so is a longer fall safer? No, because dead cats never reached the vet, so only survivors were counted, but the classic case came from war: bombers came home with holes on wings and fuselage, so the plan was to armour the holes, but the statistician saw that planes hit in the engines never came back, so armour goes where there are no holes, but the famous red-dotted plane was drawn in 2005, so ask whose stories didn't make it back.
On-screen hook: HIGHER FALL, FEWER INJURIES?

[hook] Cats that fell from higher floors seemed to get hurt less.
[hook / detail] In 1987, New York vets studied 132 cats that fell from high-rise windows.
[question / so] So is a longer fall safer, or are we missing something?
[reveal1 / answer] We're missing the cats that never reached the vet.
[reveal1 / because] A cat killed by a long fall was rarely brought in, so it never entered the data.
[reveal1 / so] That's survivorship bias: judging only by what made it through.
[reveal2 / but] But the classic case came from World War Two.
[reveal2 / detail] In 1943, a statistics group at Columbia University studied bombers that came back.
[reveal2 / detail] Their bullet holes clustered on the wings and fuselage, not the engines.
[reveal2 / so] So, the story goes, the plan was to add armour where the holes were.
[reveal3 / but] But the group's statistician saw the opposite.
[reveal3 / because] Planes hit in the engines didn't come back to be counted.
[reveal3 / so] So armour belongs exactly where the returning planes show no holes.
[turn / but] But the legend is tidier than the truth: his reports were pages of equations.
[turn / detail] The famous plane covered in red dots was drawn in 2005.
[payoff / so] So the lesson outlives the legend: for every dropout billionaire, thousands of dropouts went broke.
[payoff / so] Whose stories are you not hearing, because they never made it back?

## monty-hall (philosophy, 2026-11-27)

Angle: Why does a simple game-show puzzle fool even brilliant people?
Logline: Pigeons beat people at this puzzle, so why does it fool us? Three doors, one car, and the host shows a goat, so switching wins two times in three, but in 1990 about 10,000 readers told the columnist who said so that she was wrong, and a great mathematician held out until a simulation, because your first pick is right only a third of the time, so the host is pointing at the car, but the real Monty Hall said hosts don't play by fixed rules, so ask why you're being offered the switch.
On-screen hook: PIGEONS BEAT PEOPLE

[hook] Pigeons learn to solve this puzzle better than most people.
[hook / detail] After a month of practice, they switched 96 percent of the time; humans, about two-thirds.
[question / so] So why does a simple game-show puzzle fool so many smart people?
[reveal1 / answer] Three doors: a car behind one, goats behind the other two.
[reveal1 / detail] You pick a door; the host, who knows, opens another and shows a goat.
[reveal1 / so] Should you switch? Yes: switching wins two times in three.
[reveal2 / but] But in 1990, when a magazine columnist said so, about 10,000 readers wrote to tell her she was wrong.
[reveal2 / detail] Nearly a thousand of them had PhDs.
[reveal2 / detail] One of the century's great mathematicians refused to believe it until he saw a computer simulation.
[reveal3 / because] The trick: your first pick is right only one time in three.
[reveal3 / so] So two times in three, the car is behind the other doors, and the host never opens it.
[reveal3 / detail] Picture 100 doors: you pick one, he opens 98 goats. Would you stay?
[turn / but] But the real Monty Hall said the puzzle missed something.
[turn / detail] On his show, he didn't always offer a switch, and used offers to play with contestants' nerves.
[payoff / so] So the maths is simple only when the rules are fixed.
[payoff / so] When someone offers you a second choice, do you ask why they're offering it?
