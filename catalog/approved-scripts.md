[hook] In 2005, Britain voted this its greatest painting.
[hook / but] It shows a warship being towed away to be scrapped.
[question / so] So why do people love a picture of a hero going to its death?

## sunk-cost (philosophy, 2026-11-27)

Angle: Why do we keep going with things we've paid for, even when they make us miserable?
Logline: Most people chose the ski trip they would enjoy less because it cost more, so why? On-screen hook: THE TRIP THEY'D ENJOY LESS

[hook] In a 1985 study, most people chose the holiday they'd enjoy less.
[hook / because] It had cost them more.
[question / so] So why do we keep paying for things that make us miserable?
[reveal1 / answer] Psychologists at Ohio University posed a puzzle: you've booked two ski trips for the same weekend.
[reveal1 / detail] Michigan cost 100 dollars; Wisconsin cost 50, and sounds more fun.
[reveal1 / so] Over half chose Michigan, so as not to waste the bigger sum.
[reveal2 / but] But that was only imaginary money.
[reveal2 / so] So they sold real theatre season tickets, giving random buyers a discount.
[reveal2 / detail] Full-price buyers went to more plays, at least for the first half of the season.
[reveal2 / so] Economists call it the sunk cost fallacy: money already gone shouldn't change what you do next.
[reveal3 / but] But it isn't just a human quirk.
[reveal3 / detail] In 2018, scientists gave mice, rats and people the same kind of waiting game.
[reveal3 / detail] All three were likelier to keep waiting, the longer they had already waited.
[reveal3 / so] So the pull of a sunk cost may be older than money itself.
[turn / but] But biologists named this mistake after a jet.
[turn / detail] Britain and France kept funding Concorde long after it couldn't pay off; only fourteen ever flew passengers.
[payoff / so] So the cure is simple to say, and hard to do: only the future counts.
[payoff / so] What are you still doing just because you've already paid for it?

On-screen hook: SEWN INTO HIS COAT

[hook] When Pascal died, his servant found a note sewn inside his coat.
[hook / detail] It recorded one night in 1654, and began with a single word: Fire.
[question / so] So why did the same man call believing in God simply a smart bet?
[reveal1 / answer] That year, Pascal had helped invent the maths of chance, to settle a gambler's question.
[reveal1 / so] So he aimed it at the biggest question of all.
[reveal1 / detail] If God exists and you believe, you win an infinity of happy life.
[reveal1 / detail] If not, you've lost a few pleasures: a finite stake.
[reveal2 / so] So any chance of infinity, however small, outweighs any finite cost.
[reveal2 / detail] Historians call it the first piece of decision theory.
[reveal3 / but] But in 1746, Denis Diderot spotted the hole: an imam could make the same bet.
[reveal3 / so] With many possible gods, which door do you bet on?
[reveal3 / detail] Pick wrong, and the infinite loss is yours.
[reveal3 / but] Pascal had one more answer for doubters: act as if you believe, take holy water, and belief will follow.
[turn / but] But the wager broke loose from God.
[turn / detail] Today it's called Pascal's mugging: a tiny chance of a huge payoff can seem to justify almost anything.
[payoff / so] So the bet meant to end doubt became a warning about infinite stakes.
[payoff / so] If someone offers you infinity for a small price, should you pay?

[reveal3 / but] But utilitarians fought back: real pleasures shrink as you get more.
[reveal3 / so] Your tenth slice is worth less than your first, so sharing usually wins.

[hook / because] It has no reason to pick one over the other.
[question / so] So can a mind with no reason to choose ever choose at all?

[payoff / so] When two options are truly equal, is flipping a coin the most rational thing you can do?
