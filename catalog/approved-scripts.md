## marshmallow-test (philosophy, 2026-11-27)

Angle: Does waiting for a second marshmallow really show willpower?
Logline: One broken promise made children give up four times sooner, so what does the test measure? Mischel offered one treat now or two later, but it became famous when the waiters had better SAT scores, so it became the symbol of willpower, but Rochester showed children wait twelve minutes after a kept promise and three after a broken one, so waiting depends on trust, but a 2018 retest found the link halved and mostly explained by background, so eating now may be experience, not weakness.
On-screen hook: ONE BROKEN PROMISE

[hook] One broken promise made preschoolers give up four times sooner.
[question / so] So what does the famous marshmallow test really measure?
[reveal1 / answer] In the late 1960s, Walter Mischel tested children at Stanford's nursery school.
[reveal1 / detail] Eat one treat now, or wait alone, up to twenty minutes, for two.
[reveal1 / detail] Some covered their eyes or sang to themselves to hold out.
[reveal2 / but] But the test became famous only years later.
[reveal2 / detail] Mischel tracked down the children as teenagers.
[reveal2 / detail] Those who had waited longer had higher SAT scores and handled stress better.
[reveal2 / so] So the marshmallow became a symbol of willpower, in books and classrooms.
[reveal3 / but] But in 2012, Rochester researchers changed one thing before the test.
[reveal3 / detail] An adult promised children better crayons, then came back with them, or empty-handed.
[reveal3 / detail] Kids she had let down waited three minutes; kids she'd kept her word to waited twelve.
[reveal3 / so] So waiting only makes sense if you believe the second treat will come.
[turn / but] But in 2018, a retest on 918 more varied children found the teenage link half as strong.
[turn / detail] Account for family background, and two-thirds of it disappeared.
[payoff / so] So the child who eats it now may not lack willpower; she may have learned that promises break.
[payoff / so] When you can't wait, is it weakness, or experience?

Glaucon says nobody is good by choice, but Gyges was a real king whose rise in Herodotus came from being seen, not unseen, but would anonymity change us today? On-screen hook: INVISIBLE. WOULD YOU STAY GOOD?

[hook] A shepherd takes a ring from a corpse inside a bronze horse.
[hook / so] Turn it, and he vanishes; so he seduces the queen and kills the king.
[question / so] So if you could never be caught, would you still be good?
[reveal1 / answer] Plato put this story in his brother Glaucon's mouth, around 375 BC.
[reveal1 / detail] Glaucon's claim: nobody is good by choice, only from fear of being seen.
[reveal1 / so] Give one ring to a just man and one to a thief, and they'd act alike.
[reveal2 / but] But Gyges wasn't made up: he was a real king of Lydia, ruling from Sardis.
[reveal2 / detail] In Herodotus, his rise has no ring at all.
[reveal2 / detail] His king hid him in the bedroom to see the queen undress.
[reveal2 / so] She spotted him, so she gave him a choice: kill the king, or die.
[reveal3 / but] But does being unseen really change people?
[reveal3 / answer] In 1976, psychologists secretly watched 1,352 Seattle trick-or-treaters told to take one sweet.
[reveal3 / detail] Masked children in groups took extra 57 percent of the time.
[reveal3 / but] Asked their names and alone, fewer than one in ten did.
[turn / but] But even hidden in a crowd, many children still took just one.
[turn / so] Plato spent eight more books on why: the unjust man isn't free, he's a slave to his appetites.
[payoff / answer] So the ring doesn't change who you are; it only removes your excuse.
[payoff / so] What would you do first, and what does that say about you?

[question / so] So if a machine did that to you, would you arrive, or die?

## prisoners-dilemma (philosophy, 2026-11-27)

Logline: RAND built a game where betrayal always pays, but its test players kept cooperating, so why? On-screen hook: BETRAYAL WINS. THEY COOPERATED ANYWAY

[hook] In 1950, two RAND scientists invented a game where betrayal always pays.
[hook / but] But the colleagues they tested it on kept choosing to cooperate.
[question / so] So if betraying is the smart move, why do people cooperate?
[reveal1 / answer] Picture two prisoners in separate rooms: stay silent, or betray?
[reveal1 / detail] Both silent: one year each. Both betray: five years each.
[reveal1 / detail] Betray a silent partner, and you walk free; he gets ten.
[reveal1 / so] So whatever your partner does, betraying cuts your sentence.
[reveal2 / but] But the RAND players weren't playing once; they played a hundred rounds.
[reveal2 / detail] One cooperated 68 times, the other 78.
[reveal2 / so] So in 1980, Robert Axelrod ran a tournament of programs playing it again and again.
[reveal2 / detail] The simplest entry won, Tit for Tat: be nice first, then copy their last move.
[reveal3 / but] But the strongest proof came from soldiers in trenches.
[reveal3 / detail] In World War One, enemy units faced each other for months.
[reveal3 / so] So they made quiet deals: eight to nine a.m. was kept for private business.
[reveal3 / detail] Break the peace, and they fired back, often three shells for one.
[turn / but] But it only works while there's a next time.

[payoff / answer] So we cooperate because life is rarely played once.
[payoff / but] But if this were your last round with someone, would you keep your word?
