## utility-monster (philosophy, 2026-11-27)

Angle: Should whoever enjoys something most always get the most of it?
Logline: One creature enjoys cake a thousand times more, so should it get every slice? Utilitarianism adds up happiness and counts each person as one, but Nozick's utility monster gains more from every sacrifice, so the theory feeds it everything, but pleasures usually shrink as you get more, so sharing wins, but a true monster never gets full, so Parfit weighted the worse off, but digital minds may make the monster real, so it becomes a design choice.
On-screen hook: 1,000x THE PLEASURE

[hook] One creature enjoys cake a thousand times more than anyone else.
[hook / so] By one famous moral rule, it should get every slice.
[question / so] So should whoever enjoys something most always get the most?
[reveal1 / answer] The rule is utilitarianism: do whatever creates the most total happiness.
[reveal1 / detail] Its founder, Jeremy Bentham, insisted everybody counts for one, nobody for more.
[reveal2 / but] But in 1974, Robert Nozick invented a creature to break it: the utility monster.
[reveal2 / detail] It gains more from any sacrifice of ours than we lose.
[reveal2 / so] So the theory says: feed it everything, even if the rest of us go hungry.
[reveal2 / because] Everyone still counts for one; the monster just turns each slice into more joy.
[reveal3 / but] But utilitarians fought back: real pleasures shrink as you get more.
[reveal3 / so] Your tenth slice is worth less than your first, so sharing usually wins.
[reveal3 / but] But a true monster never gets full; that's the whole point.
[reveal3 / so] So Derek Parfit proposed giving extra weight to whoever is worse off.
[turn / but] But the monster may stop being imaginary.
[turn / detail] In 2020, two philosophers warned that digital minds could be built to feel far more than we do.
[payoff / so] So the monster is no longer a joke; it could be a design choice.
[payoff / so] If we built a mind that enjoys life a million times more than you, should it come first?

## buridans-donkey (philosophy, 2026-11-27)

Angle: Can a mind with no reason to choose ever choose at all?
Logline: A perfectly logical donkey between two equal piles would starve, so can a mind choose without a reason? The puzzle carries a Paris philosopher's name, but he never wrote it, because critics made it up to mock him, but the idea is older: Aristotle's starving man, and Spinoza agreed he would die, but Leibniz said balance never happens, but in 1984 Lamport found the donkey inside every chip, so engineers can make the stall rare but never impossible, but journals rejected him for decades, so real choosers survive by cheating.
On-screen hook: TWO EQUAL PILES. NO CHOICE.

[hook] A perfectly logical donkey, between two identical piles of hay, would starve.
[hook / because] It has no reason to pick one over the other.
[question / so] So can a mind with no reason to choose ever choose at all?
[reveal1 / answer] The puzzle carries the name of a 14th-century Paris philosopher.
[reveal1 / but] But the donkey never appears in his surviving writings.
[reveal1 / because] Critics likely invented it to mock his view that the will must follow reason.
[reveal2 / but] But the idea is far older.
[reveal2 / detail] Aristotle pictured a man as hungry as he was thirsty, frozen between food and drink.
[reveal2 / detail] In 1677, Spinoza agreed such a man would die, and said he didn't know if he was a man or an ass.
[reveal2 / but] Leibniz replied that perfect balance never happens: some tiny difference always tips you.
[reveal3 / but] But in 1984, computer scientist Leslie Lamport found the donkey inside every computer.
[reveal3 / detail] A chip deciding whether a signal came before or after a clock tick can stick in between.
[reveal3 / detail] Engineers call it metastability, and it can crash a system.
[reveal3 / so] So they make the stall astronomically rare, but never impossible.
[turn / but] But journals rejected Lamport's paper; it was finally published in 2012.
[payoff / so] So real choosers survive by cheating: a coin flip, a whim, a tiny bias.
[payoff / so] When two options are truly equal, is flipping a coin the most rational thing you can do?

## sorites (philosophy, 2026-11-27)

Angle: Where exactly does a heap begin?
Logline: One grain is no heap and one more never makes one, so where does a heap begin? Each step looks safe, so either heaps don't exist or one grain decides, but it isn't about sand: baldness, adulthood, colour all blur, so Chrysippus advised falling silent, but Williamson argued a sharp line exists that we can never know, but engineers bet on blur and ran a subway on degrees of truth, so the real question is who draws the line.
On-screen hook: ONE GRAIN AT A TIME

[hook] One grain of sand isn't a heap. Neither are two.
[hook / so] Add one grain at a time, and by that logic, a million grains still aren't a heap.
[question / so] So where exactly does a heap begin?
[reveal1 / answer] Greek logicians asked this 2,400 years ago; sorites means heap.
[reveal1 / detail] The same thinker is credited with the liar paradox: this sentence is false.
[reveal1 / detail] Each step looks safe: one grain can't turn a non-heap into a heap.
[reveal1 / so] So either there are no heaps at all, or one grain makes all the difference.
[reveal2 / but] But this was never really about sand.
[reveal2 / detail] When does a man go bald, or does red turn orange in a rainbow?
[reveal2 / so] The Stoic Chrysippus advised simply falling silent as you near the border.
[reveal3 / but] But in 1994, Timothy Williamson defended the shocking option.
[reveal3 / detail] There is a sharp line, he argued: one exact grain makes the heap.
[reveal3 / but] We just can never know which grain it is.
[reveal3 / but] It sounds absurd, but it's the only answer that keeps ordinary logic intact.
[turn / but] But engineers bet on blur instead.
[turn / detail] In 1987, Sendai's subway began braking by fuzzy logic: a little, quite, very.
[payoff / so] So maybe truth comes in degrees, and the real question isn't where the line is.
[payoff / so] Eighteen to vote, one grain for a heap: who gets to draw your lines?

Angle: Would you go along with a crowd that is plainly wrong?
Logline: Three in four students denied their own eyes, so would you go along with a wrong crowd? On-screen hook: WHICH LINE MATCHES?

[hook] In 1951, three in four students denied their own eyes.
[hook / detail] The question was simple: which of three lines matches this one?
[question / so] So would you go along with a crowd that's plainly wrong?
[reveal1 / answer] At Swarthmore College, a psychologist sat each student with a group of others.
[reveal1 / detail] Secretly, the others were actors, told to give the same wrong answer.
[reveal1 / so] So the real subject answered near the end, after hearing them all.
[reveal2 / but] But the psychologist expected the opposite: he set out to show that people stand firm.
[reveal2 / detail] Instead, on the rigged rounds, about a third of answers followed the crowd.
[reveal2 / but] Alone, people got it wrong less than once in a hundred.
[reveal3 / but] But one small change nearly broke the spell.
[reveal3 / detail] When a single actor gave the right answer, conformity fell to about one answer in twenty.
[reveal3 / so] So you don't need a majority to trust your eyes; you need one ally.
[turn / but] But most retellings leave out the biggest number.
[turn / detail] Two-thirds of all answers were right, and a quarter of people never caved once.
[payoff / so] So a crowd can bend us, but it rarely breaks us, especially when one person speaks first.
[payoff / so] Next time a room is wrong, will you be that one person?

On-screen hook: 38 WITNESSES. OR WERE THERE?

[hook] A 1964 headline claimed 38 neighbours watched a murder and did nothing.
[hook / so] That story launched one of psychology's most famous ideas.
[question / so] So does a crowd really make you less likely to help?
[reveal1 / answer] Kitty Genovese was attacked outside her apartment in Queens, at three in the morning.
[reveal1 / detail] The New York Times said 38 people saw or heard it, and nobody called.
[reveal1 / so] So two New York psychologists went looking for the cause.

[reveal2 / because] Each person's share of the responsibility shrank as the crowd grew.
[reveal3 / but] But the Genovese story itself was wrong.

[turn / detail] In 2019, researchers studied CCTV of 219 fights in Amsterdam, Lancaster and Cape Town; in nine of ten, someone stepped in.

[payoff / so] Next time you're one of many, will you be the one who moves first?

On-screen hook: ONE BROKEN PROMISE

[hook] One broken promise made preschoolers give up four times sooner.
[question / so] So what does the famous marshmallow test really measure?
[reveal1 / answer] In the late 1960s, Walter Mischel tested children at Stanford's nursery school.
[reveal1 / detail] Eat one treat now, or wait alone, up to twenty minutes, for two.
[reveal1 / detail] Some covered their eyes or sang to themselves to hold out.
[reveal2 / but] But the test became famous only years later.
[reveal2 / detail] Mischel tracked down the children as teenagers.
[reveal2 / detail] Those who had waited longer had higher SAT scores and handled stress better.
[reveal2 / so] So the marshmallow became a symbol of willpower, in books and classrooms.
[reveal3 / but] But in 2012, Rochester researchers changed one thing before the test.
[reveal3 / detail] An adult promised children better crayons, then came back with them, or empty-handed.

[reveal3 / so] So waiting only makes sense if you believe the second treat will come.
[turn / but] But in 2018, a retest on 918 more varied children found the teenage link half as strong.
[turn / detail] Account for family background, and two-thirds of it disappeared.
[payoff / so] So the child who eats it now may not lack willpower; she may have learned that promises break.
[payoff / so] When you can't wait, is it weakness, or experience?
