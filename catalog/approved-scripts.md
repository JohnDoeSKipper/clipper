## sorites (philosophy, 2026-11-27)

Angle: Where exactly does a heap begin?
Logline: One grain is no heap and one more never makes one, so where does a heap begin? Each step looks safe, so either heaps don't exist or one grain decides, but it isn't about sand: baldness, adulthood, colour all blur, so Chrysippus advised falling silent, but Williamson argued a sharp line exists that we can never know, but engineers bet on blur and ran a subway on degrees of truth, so the real question is who draws the line.
On-screen hook: ONE GRAIN AT A TIME

[hook] One grain of sand isn't a heap. Neither are two.
[hook / so] Add one grain at a time, and by that logic, a million grains still aren't a heap.
[question / so] So where exactly does a heap begin?
[reveal1 / answer] Greek logicians asked this 2,400 years ago; sorites means heap.
[reveal1 / detail] The same thinker is credited with the liar paradox: this sentence is false.
[reveal1 / detail] Each step looks safe: one grain can't turn a non-heap into a heap.
[reveal1 / so] So either there are no heaps at all, or one grain makes all the difference.
[reveal2 / but] But this was never really about sand.
[reveal2 / detail] When does a man go bald, or does red turn orange in a rainbow?
[reveal2 / so] The Stoic Chrysippus advised simply falling silent as you near the border.
[reveal3 / but] But in 1994, Timothy Williamson defended the shocking option.
[reveal3 / detail] There is a sharp line, he argued: one exact grain makes the heap.
[reveal3 / but] We just can never know which grain it is.
[reveal3 / but] It sounds absurd, but it's the only answer that keeps ordinary logic intact.
[turn / but] But engineers bet on blur instead.
[turn / detail] In 1987, Sendai's subway began braking by fuzzy logic: a little, quite, very.
[payoff / so] So maybe truth comes in degrees, and the real question isn't where the line is.
[payoff / so] Eighteen to vote, one grain for a heap: who gets to draw your lines?

On-screen hook: BOUGHT BY THE KING. THEN HIDDEN.

[hook] France's new king bought this painting, then hid it away.
[hook / but] Today it stands for France; for years it was too dangerous to hang.
[question / so] So why did France hide the painting that now stands for it?
[reveal1 / answer] It isn't 1789: it's July 1830, when Paris toppled Charles the Tenth.
[reveal1 / detail] Delacroix didn't fight; at least I'll paint for her, he told his brother.
[reveal1 / detail] Behind the smoke: Notre-Dame, flying the tricolour.
[reveal2 / but] But this Liberty is no marble goddess.
[reveal2 / detail] She's a barefoot woman of the streets, striding over the dead.
[reveal2 / detail] Beside her, a boy with two pistols, perhaps the model for Hugo's Gavroche.
[reveal3 / but] But the revolution had crowned a new king, Louis-Philippe.
[reveal3 / so] His government bought it at the 1831 Salon for 3,000 francs.
[reveal3 / but] Then, after a Paris uprising in 1832, it vanished into storage.
[reveal3 / because] Cheering a king's fall set a bad example for his subjects.
[turn / but] But in 1874, the new Republic hung it in the Louvre.
[turn / detail] Later still, it went onto the 100-franc note.
[payoff / so] So the painting a king hid became the face of the republic.

## asch-conformity (philosophy, 2026-11-27)

Angle: Would you go along with a crowd that is plainly wrong?
Logline: Three in four students denied their own eyes, so would you go along with a wrong crowd? The others were secret actors, so the subject heard them all first, but the psychologist expected people to stand firm, and a third of rigged answers followed the crowd, but one honest ally cut that to one in twenty, so you need one friend, not a majority, but the retelling skips the biggest number: two-thirds of answers were right, so a crowd bends us but rarely breaks us.
On-screen hook: WHICH LINE MATCHES?

[hook] In 1951, three in four students denied their own eyes.
[hook / detail] The question was simple: which of three lines matches this one?
[question / so] So would you go along with a crowd that's plainly wrong?
[reveal1 / answer] At Swarthmore College, a psychologist sat each student with a group of others.
[reveal1 / detail] Secretly, the others were actors, told to give the same wrong answer.
[reveal1 / so] So the real subject answered near the end, after hearing them all.
[reveal2 / but] But the psychologist expected the opposite: he set out to show that people stand firm.
[reveal2 / detail] Instead, on the rigged rounds, about a third of answers followed the crowd.
[reveal2 / but] Alone, people got it wrong less than once in a hundred.
[reveal3 / but] But one small change nearly broke the spell.
[reveal3 / detail] When a single actor gave the right answer, conformity fell to about one answer in twenty.
[reveal3 / so] So you don't need a majority to trust your eyes; you need one ally.
[turn / but] But most retellings leave out the biggest number.
[turn / detail] Two-thirds of all answers were right, and a quarter of people never caved once.
[payoff / so] So a crowd can bend us, but it rarely breaks us, especially when one person speaks first.
[payoff / so] Next time a room is wrong, will you be that one person?

## bystander-effect (philosophy, 2026-11-27)

Angle: Does a crowd really make people less likely to help?
Logline: A 1964 headline said 38 neighbours watched a murder and did nothing, so does a crowd stop help? two New York psychologists blamed numbers, not cold hearts, because their seizure study saw help fall from 85% to 31% with four listeners, but the Genovese story was wrong, because few saw it and a friend ran to her, but the strongest test came from real CCTV, which found someone helps in nine of ten fights, so crowds don't stop help, they only make each of us wait.
On-screen hook: 38 WITNESSES. OR WERE THERE?

[hook] A 1964 headline claimed 38 neighbours watched a murder and did nothing.
[hook / so] That story launched one of psychology's most famous ideas.
[question / so] So does a crowd really make you less likely to help?
[reveal1 / answer] Kitty Genovese was attacked outside her apartment in Queens, at three in the morning.
[reveal1 / detail] The New York Times said 38 people saw or heard it, and nobody called.
[reveal1 / so] So two New York psychologists went looking for the cause.
[reveal2 / but] But they didn't blame cold city hearts; they blamed numbers.
[reveal2 / because] In 1968, students heard a fellow student have a seizure over an intercom.
[reveal2 / detail] Alone, 85 percent ran for help; believing four others could hear, only 31 percent did.
[reveal2 / because] Each person's share of the responsibility shrank as the crowd grew.
[reveal3 / but] But the Genovese story itself was wrong.
[reveal3 / because] Only a handful saw the attack, and one man's shout drove the killer off.
[reveal3 / detail] Her friend Sophia Farrar ran out and held her as she died.
[turn / but] But the best test came from real street cameras.
[turn / detail] In 2019, researchers studied CCTV of 219 fights in Amsterdam, Lancaster and Cape Town; in nine of ten, someone stepped in.
[payoff / so] So crowds don't stop help; they make each of us wait for someone else.
[payoff / so] Next time you're one of many, will you be the one who moves first?

## marshmallow-test (philosophy, 2026-11-27)

Angle: Does waiting for a second marshmallow really show willpower?
Logline: One broken promise made children give up four times sooner, so what does the test measure? Mischel offered one treat now or two later, but it became famous when the waiters had better SAT scores, so it became the symbol of willpower, but Rochester showed children wait twelve minutes after a kept promise and three after a broken one, so waiting depends on trust, but a 2018 retest found the link halved and mostly explained by background, so eating now may be experience, not weakness.
On-screen hook: ONE BROKEN PROMISE

[hook] One broken promise made preschoolers give up four times sooner.
[question / so] So what does the famous marshmallow test really measure?
[reveal1 / answer] In the late 1960s, Walter Mischel tested children at Stanford's nursery school.
[reveal1 / detail] Eat one treat now, or wait alone, up to twenty minutes, for two.
[reveal1 / detail] Some covered their eyes or sang to themselves to hold out.
[reveal2 / but] But the test became famous only years later.
[reveal2 / detail] Mischel tracked down the children as teenagers.
[reveal2 / detail] Those who had waited longer had higher SAT scores and handled stress better.
[reveal2 / so] So the marshmallow became a symbol of willpower, in books and classrooms.
[reveal3 / but] But in 2012, Rochester researchers changed one thing before the test.
[reveal3 / detail] An adult promised children better crayons, then came back with them, or empty-handed.
[reveal3 / detail] Kids she had let down waited three minutes; kids she'd kept her word to waited twelve.
[reveal3 / so] So waiting only makes sense if you believe the second treat will come.
[turn / but] But in 2018, a retest on 918 more varied children found the teenage link half as strong.
[turn / detail] Account for family background, and two-thirds of it disappeared.
[payoff / so] So the child who eats it now may not lack willpower; she may have learned that promises break.
[payoff / so] When you can't wait, is it weakness, or experience?

On-screen hook: LENT ONCE. INTO A FIRE.

[hook] This painting has left Chicago once, and that museum caught fire.
[hook / but] It escaped the 1958 blaze, but it was already fading.
[question / so] So why is Seurat's sunny afternoon darker than the one he painted?

[reveal2 / but] But it took him two years and dozens of studies.
[reveal2 / detail] Around 1889, he even painted a border of dots around the edge.

In 1976 masked children in groups stole far more, but most still took one, so Plato spent eight books arguing injustice enslaves you, therefore the ring only removes your excuse.
On-screen hook: INVISIBLE. WOULD YOU STAY GOOD?

[hook] A shepherd takes a ring from a corpse inside a bronze horse.
[hook / so] Turn it, and he vanishes; so he seduces the queen and kills the king.
[question / so] So if you could never be caught, would you still be good?
[reveal1 / answer] Plato put this story in his brother Glaucon's mouth, around 375 BC.
[reveal1 / detail] Glaucon's claim: nobody is good by choice, only from fear of being seen.
[reveal1 / so] Give one ring to a just man and one to a thief, and they'd act alike.
[reveal2 / but] But Gyges wasn't made up: he was a real king of Lydia, ruling from Sardis.
[reveal2 / detail] In Herodotus, his rise has no ring at all.
[reveal2 / detail] His king hid him in the bedroom to see the queen undress.
[reveal2 / so] She spotted him, so she gave him a choice: kill the king, or die.
[reveal3 / but] But does being unseen really change people?
[reveal3 / answer] In 1976, psychologists secretly watched 1,352 Seattle trick-or-treaters told to take one sweet.
[reveal3 / detail] Masked children in groups took extra 57 percent of the time.
[reveal3 / but] Asked their names and alone, fewer than one in ten did.
[turn / but] But even hidden in a crowd, many children still took just one.
[turn / so] Plato spent eight more books on why: the unjust man isn't free, he's a slave to his appetites.
[payoff / answer] So the ring doesn't change who you are; it only removes your excuse.
[payoff / so] What would you do first, and what does that say about you?

## teleporter (philosophy, 2026-11-27)

Angle: If a machine destroyed you here and rebuilt you perfectly on Mars, would you arrive, or die?
Logline: Real teleportation destroys the original, so would a teleporter kill you? Parfit's machine rebuilds you on Mars from new matter, but then he made it copy without destroying, so two of you exist and one is dying, but he argued the question itself is the mistake, because split-brain surgery shows one person could become two, so identity is not what matters, but philosophers are still split, so you would arrive in every way that matters, unless you also distrust waking up.
On-screen hook: THE ORIGINAL IS DESTROYED

[hook] Physicists really do teleport particles, and the original is always destroyed.
[hook / because] Physics forbids a perfect copy, so the original has to go.
[question / so] So if a machine did that to you, would you arrive, or die?
[reveal1 / answer] Philosopher Derek Parfit built that machine on paper in 1984.
[reveal1 / detail] A scanner records every cell of your body, then destroys it.
[reveal1 / so] On Mars, a replicator rebuilds you from new matter.
[reveal2 / but] But then Parfit changed the scanner: it copies you without destroying you.
[reveal2 / detail] On Earth, you learn the scan damaged your heart: you'll die in days.
[reveal2 / detail] On a screen, you watch yourself on Mars, starting to speak.
[reveal2 / so] So which one is you: the one dying, or the one who lives?
[reveal3 / but] But Parfit argued the question itself is the mistake.
[reveal3 / because] Surgeons can cut the bridge between the brain's halves, and each half acts alone.
[reveal3 / so] Put each half in a new body, and both would remember being you.
[reveal3 / so] So identity, he concluded, isn't what matters; your memories and character carrying on is.
[turn / but] But philosophers are split: 36 percent say you'd survive it, 31 percent say you'd die.
[turn / detail] That's from a 2020 survey of 931 professors.
[payoff / answer] So if he's right, you'd arrive, in every way that matters.
[payoff / but] But sleep breaks your stream of thought nightly: why trust waking up, but not the machine?
