## milgram (philosophy, 2026-11-27)

Angle: Would you hurt someone just because an authority told you to?
Logline: Two in three pressed the 450-volt switch, so would you hurt someone on command? Milgram's 1961 Yale study used an actor and fake shocks, asking the Eichmann question, but obedience wasn't fixed: a shabby office, a phone, rebel peers all cut it, but the strongest clue was the words, because people stopped when told they had no choice, so they were serving science, not orders, but Milgram's files show many doubted the shocks, so it shows how far we go for something we trust.
On-screen hook: THE MAN NEXT DOOR WENT QUIET

[hook] Two out of three people pressed the switch marked 450 volts.
[hook / detail] The man screaming next door had already gone silent.
[question / so] So would you hurt someone just because an authority told you to?
[reveal1 / answer] In 1961, Yale psychologist Stanley Milgram recruited 40 men for a memory study.
[reveal1 / detail] Each shocked a learner for wrong answers, 15 volts higher each time.
[reveal1 / but] The learner was an actor and the shocks were fake, but the volunteers didn't know.
[reveal1 / because] Milgram was testing a question from the Eichmann trial, then under way in Jerusalem.
[reveal2 / but] But obedience wasn't fixed.
[reveal2 / detail] In a shabby office in Bridgeport instead of Yale, it fell to 48 percent.
[reveal2 / detail] With orders by phone, about 20; with two fellow teachers refusing, just 10.
[reveal3 / but] But the strongest clue was in the words.
[reveal3 / detail] The scientist's prods ran from please continue, to you have no other choice.
[reveal3 / but] Later analysis found that when told they had no choice, people stopped.
[reveal3 / so] So they weren't simply obeying; they were serving a cause they believed in: science.
[turn / but] But in 2012, a researcher reading Milgram's files found many volunteers doubted the shocks were real.
[payoff / so] So it shows less blind obedience than how far we'll go for something we trust.
[payoff / so] What cause would get you to press that switch?

## laplaces-demon (philosophy, 2026-11-27)

Angle: If a mind knew every particle in the universe, could it predict what you'll do next?
Logline: In 1814 Laplace imagined a mind that foresees everything, so is your future written? He said an intellect knowing every particle would see the future like the past, but in 1961 a rounded weather simulation diverged completely, so tiny errors explode, but the demon would need perfect data, and Heisenberg showed physics forbids it, but the strangest limit is logical: no predictor can predict itself, so the future may be fixed but no mind inside it can read it all.
On-screen hook: A MIND THAT KNOWS EVERYTHING

[hook] In 1814, a mathematician imagined a mind that could foresee everything.
[hook / detail] Including what you'll do next.
[question / so] So if the universe runs like clockwork, is your future already written?
[reveal1 / answer] Pierre-Simon Laplace wrote that a mind knowing every particle and force would see the future like the past.
[reveal1 / detail] Later writers called it Laplace's demon.
[reveal1 / detail] Asked by Napoleon where God fitted in, he reportedly said: I had no need of that hypothesis.
[reveal2 / but] But in 1961, a weather simulation broke the dream.
[reveal2 / detail] To save time, a meteorologist re-ran it with numbers rounded from six decimals to three.
[reveal2 / so] Within simulated weeks, the weather was completely different.
[reveal2 / so] Tiny errors explode: the famous butterfly in Brazil that sets off a tornado in Texas.
[reveal3 / but] But the demon would also need perfect measurements, and physics forbids them.
[reveal3 / because] In 1927, Werner Heisenberg showed you can't know a particle's position and momentum exactly at once.
[reveal3 / so] So even a perfect mind would start from a blur.
[turn / but] But the strangest limit is pure logic: in 2008, a physicist proved no predictor can predict everything.
[turn / because] Inside the universe, it would have to predict its own predictions, forever.
[payoff / so] So the future might be fixed, but no mind inside it can ever read all of it.
[payoff / so] If no one could ever predict your choice, would it matter whether it was written?

[question / so] So do you take both boxes, or just the closed one?

[reveal2 / but] But people who take one box almost always walk away millionaires.

[reveal3 / but] Among professional philosophers in 2020, two boxes led, 39 to 31.

[payoff / so] So the real question isn't the money; it's whether a choice that can be predicted is still yours.
[payoff / so] One box, or two?

[hook] By this logic, the fastest Greek hero never catches a tortoise.

[question / so] So where exactly does the argument go wrong?

[reveal2 / so] So the real puzzle is what's wrong with the steps.

[payoff / so] So Zeno lost the race, and still shaped the maths of infinity.
[payoff / so] If you can never finish an infinite list, how do you ever cross a room?
